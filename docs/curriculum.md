# IUPAC Organic Nomenclature — Curriculum Sequence

A teaching order for organic nomenclature, from first principles to advanced
naming. 38 units across 10 stages.

This document is about **what to teach and in what order, and why**. It contains
no implementation detail.

---

## Design principles

These emerged from building the full sequence and are worth preserving whatever
the delivery format.

**1. The seniority ladder is the spine.**
Nearly every naming decision reduces to "which group outranks which." Once a
learner holds the ladder, new functional groups slot into an existing structure
rather than arriving as isolated facts. Sequence the whole course around it.

**2. Teach priority explicitly, and *before* it is needed.**
The single most important structural decision: a dedicated unit on
functional-group priority sits at unit 10 — after the first two competing groups
(alcohols and carbonyls) exist, but before the pile-up begins. Learners who meet
priority only implicitly, group by group, never build the general rule.

**3. Bidirectional throughout.**
Every unit teaches both structure → name and name → structure. These are
different skills; fluency in one does not transfer automatically. Naming is
recognition; drawing is construction, and it is where misconceptions surface.

**4. One new idea per unit, reusing the last.**
Each unit introduces a single naming concept and exercises the previous one as
its substrate. Ketones after aldehydes reuse carbonyl recognition; acids after
both reuse it again and add the ladder.

**5. Introduce demotion prefixes with their parent group.**
`hydroxy-` is taught with alcohols' first defeat, `oxo-` with ketones', `amino-`
with amines'. Teaching prefixes as a separate topic makes them feel arbitrary;
teaching them as "what happens when your group loses" makes them inevitable.

**6. Group the locant-free suffixes conceptually.**
`-al`, `-oic acid`, `-amide`, `-nitrile`, `-oyl halide` all share one reason for
carrying no locant: the group can only sit at a chain end, so its carbon is C-1
by definition. Learners who see this once stop asking about it five times.

**7. Counter-examples are content.**
Include the cases that *don't* work: the alkene with no cis/trans forms, the
alcohol with no chiral centre, the carbonyl that can't be a ketone. Negative
examples define a boundary far better than more positive ones.

**8. Isomerism goes late.**
Distinguishing isomers requires enough naming fluency to *express* the
distinction. Attempting it early produces hand-waving.

## Stage 1 · Foundations (units 1–3)

Establish that molecules have systematic names and that skeletal drawings encode
structure. Nothing competes for priority yet, so all attention goes on reading
structures and counting carbons.

**1. Alkanes and the carbon count** — meth/eth/prop/but through dec; the
`-ane` suffix; general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Reading skeletal notation: line ends and
vertices are carbons, hydrogens are implied.

**2. Straight-chain naming** — mapping a drawn chain to a root and back.
Molecular formulas from structures.

**3. Finding the parent chain** — the longest continuous chain, including when
it bends across the page. This is the first real skill, and the first place
learners fail: they read the chain as drawn rather than as connected.

*Examples: methane → decane, hexane drawn bent, pentane vs. its drawn shape.*

---

## Stage 2 · Branching (units 4–6)

**4. Alkyl substituents** — methyl, ethyl, propyl, butyl. Naming a branch as a
modification of a parent.

**5. Numbering and locants** — the lowest-locant rule. Why direction matters and
how to choose it.

**6. Multiple substituents** — di/tri/tetra multipliers; alphabetical citation;
lowest-locant *sets* compared at the first point of difference; multiplying
prefixes ignored for alphabetisation.

*Examples: 2-methylbutane, 2,3-dimethylbutane, 3-ethyl-2-methylpentane,
2,2,4-trimethylpentane.*

---

## Stage 3 · Unsaturation and halogens (units 7–8)

**7. Alkenes and alkynes** — `-ene` and `-yne`; the locant names the *first*
carbon of the multiple bond; the parent chain must contain the multiple bond
even when a longer chain exists without it. First taste of priority: the
multiple bond outranks alkyl branches for numbering.

**8. Haloalkanes** — fluoro/chloro/bromo/iodo as prefixes. Critically: halogens
have *no* suffix form and never outrank anything. This is the cleanest possible
introduction to the idea that groups have ranks.

*Examples: but-2-ene, pent-1-yne, buta-1,3-diene, 2-chlorobutane,
1,2-dichloroethane.*

---

## Stage 4 · Oxygen and the ladder (units 9–16)

The heart of the course. Alcohols create the first genuine priority conflict;
unit 10 then generalises it; the rest of the stage populates the ladder.

**9. Alcohols** — `-ol` with locants; `-diol`; the retained `e` before a
consonant (`pentane-2,4-diol` vs `pentan-2-ol`). First group to beat a multiple
bond for numbering.

**10. Functional-group priority** ⭐ — the pivotal unit. The full ladder stated
explicitly. One suffix per name. Outranked groups become prefixes: `hydroxy-`,
`oxo-`. Tie-breaking down the ladder when the principal group ties from both
ends.

**11. Aldehydes** — `-al`, locant-free because the group can only be terminal.
Numbering is forced from the carbonyl end, so substituents take whatever locants
remain. `-enal` for unsaturated cases.

**12. Ketones** — `-one` *with* a locant. Why the smallest ketone is
propan-2-one, and why a "butan-1-one" is impossible (it would be an aldehyde).
`-dione`.

**13. Carboxylic acids** — `-oic acid`, top of the ladder, locant-free. Hydroxy
acids (lactic acid) as the standard demotion case.

**14. Esters** — the two-word name; the acyl half is the parent, the alkyl half
comes first in the name but is not numbered. Swapped-half errors
(methyl propanoate vs propyl ethanoate) are the diagnostic mistake here.

**15. Acyl halides** — `-oyl chloride`. Distinguishing a halogen that is *part of
the functional group* from a halogen substituent elsewhere.

**16. Acid anhydrides** — symmetric only. The distinguishing test versus esters:
count the carbonyls flanking the bridging oxygen (two vs one).

*Examples: butan-2-ol, pent-4-en-2-ol, 4-hydroxybutan-2-one, butanal,
3-methylbutanal, pentan-2-one, pentane-2,4-dione, ethanoic acid,
2-hydroxypropanoic acid, ethyl ethanoate, propanoyl chloride, ethanoic
anhydride.*

---

## Stage 5 · Nitro and ethers (units 17–18)

Deliberately placed here as a breather between two dense stages. Both are
prefix-only groups and reuse Stage 2 skills.

**17. Nitro compounds** — `nitro-` is a permanent prefix, like the halogens. No
suffix form ever. Positional isomerism (1- vs 2-nitropropane).

**18. Ethers** — the smaller side plus its oxygen becomes an `alkoxy-` prefix;
the larger carbon chain is the parent; the bridging oxygen never counts in the
parent. Introduce **functional-group isomerism** here: methoxyethane and
propan-1-ol are both C₃H₈O. This plants a seed harvested in unit 29.

---

## Stage 6 · Nitrogen (units 19–21)

Placed after oxygen so the ladder already exists and nitrogen groups can be
slotted into it rather than taught in isolation.

**19. Amines** — `-amine` with locants, ranking just below alcohols; demoting to
`amino-`. Preview the amino-acid pattern.

**20. Amides** — `-amide`, locant-free. The whole unit turns on one
discrimination: **amide vs amine**, decided by whether a carbonyl sits on the
nitrogen-bearing carbon. This is the most common confusion in the course; face
it directly.

**21. Nitriles** — `-nitrile`, and the counting trap: the ≡N carbon is part of
the parent chain, so ethanenitrile has two carbons. Demotes to `cyano-`.

---

## Stage 7 · Multifunctional molecules (units 22–25)

No new groups. Entirely about combining what exists — which is a distinct skill
and deserves its own stage rather than being assumed.

**22. Alcohols with other groups** — `-OH` against halogens (wins), against
carbonyls and acids (loses). Hydroxy acids.

**23. Carbonyl combinations** — equal-rank pairs sharing a suffix (`-dial`,
`-dione`) versus mixed ranks producing `oxo-`. Oxo acids.

**24. Nitrogen and oxygen together** — the amino-acid pattern: glycine, alanine,
β-alanine. Amino alcohols.

**25. Full multifunctional** — three or more features on one chain. The
four-step routine made explicit: principal group → parent → numbering →
alphabetised prefixes. Unsaturation folded in alongside functional groups.

*Examples: 3-hydroxybutanoic acid, 3-oxobutanal, pentane-2,4-dione,
2-aminopropanoic acid, 3-aminopropan-1-ol, 2-amino-3-hydroxypropanoic acid,
2-chloro-3-hydroxybutanoic acid, 4-hydroxypent-2-enal.*

---

## Stage 8 · Rings and aromatics (units 26–28)

**26. Cycloalkanes** — the `cyclo-` prefix; a single substituent needs no
locant; the principal group defines C-1; exocyclic acid and aldehyde groups
(`-carboxylic acid`, `-carbaldehyde`).

**27. Benzene and aromatics** — the aromatic ring; single substituents without
locants; 1,2/1,3/1,4 and their ortho/meta/para names; the retained parents
**phenol, aniline, benzoic acid, benzaldehyde**.

**28. Aromatic functional groups** — numbering anchored at the group that names
the ring; why a suffix-worthy group "promotes" benzene to a retained parent
(nitrobenzene vs 4-nitrophenol).

---

## Stage 9 · Isomerism and stereochemistry (units 29–33)

Deliberately late. Every distinction here is *expressed through naming*, so it
requires the preceding fluency.

**29. Constitutional isomers** — chain, positional and functional-group
isomerism. The organising insight: **if two structures generate different
systematic names, they are different compounds**. This retroactively justifies
the whole course.

**30. Cis/trans isomerism** — restricted rotation about C=C. The test: each
alkene carbon must carry two different groups. Include alkenes that *fail* the
test. First encounter with a name that does not fully specify a compound.

**31. E/Z nomenclature** — where cis/trans becomes ambiguous; CIP priority rules
(atomic number first, then walking outward at the first point of difference);
Z = higher priorities together, E = opposite.

**32. Chiral centres** — four different groups on one carbon; enantiomers;
systematic scanning for chiral centres, with the quick disqualifiers (CH₃ and
CH₂ are never chiral). Include achiral controls such as propan-2-ol.

**33. R/S configuration** — reusing CIP from unit 31; rank all four, point the
lowest away, read 1→2→3. The classic error — forgetting to reverse when the
lowest priority faces the viewer — deserves explicit treatment.

> **Delivery note.** If the drawing tool records connectivity only, units 30–33
> cannot be assessed by drawing. Teach and test them through explanation and
> selection instead, and use their underlying skeletons as drawing practice with
> hints that frame the stereochemical point.

---

## Stage 10 · Advanced nomenclature (units 34–38)

**34. Complex substituents** — parent choice under pressure; lowest-locant sets;
alphabetisation of complex names; branched substituent names (isopropyl =
propan-2-yl, tert-butyl = 2-methylpropan-2-yl).

**35. Fused and bridged rings** — shared-edge systems (naphthalene, decalin);
ring-junction carbons; reading `bicyclo[x.y.z]`, where total ring carbons =
x + y + z + 2, and a trailing zero means fusion.

**36. Spiro compounds** — rings sharing exactly one atom; `spiro[x.y]`, where
total = x + y + 1. The one-shared-atom vs two-shared-atoms test cleanly
separates spiro from fused.

**37. Heterocycles** — pyridine, piperidine, pyrrole, furan, thiophene;
heteroatom always at position 1; two heteroatoms take the lowest locant set
(pyrimidine 1,3; pyrazine 1,4).

**38. Mastery review** — no new content. A mixed pool sampling every family,
plus explicit treatment of the recurring traps: numbering from the wrong end,
writing locants on locant-free suffixes, and the look-alike families (amide/amine,
ester/ether, acyl halide/haloalkane).

## Dependency map

Hard prerequisites — these orderings cannot be swapped:

- **3 → everything.** Parent-chain identification underlies every later unit.
- **5 → 7.** Locants before multiple-bond locants.
- **9 → 10.** A priority conflict must exist before the general rule lands.
- **10 → 11–25.** The ladder must precede every group that competes on it.
- **11 → 12.** Terminal carbonyl before interior carbonyl.
- **13 → 14–16.** Acids before their derivatives.
- **18 → 29.** Functional-group isomerism is seeded, then formalised.
- **19 → 20.** Amines before amides, so the carbonyl is the *only* new variable.
- **26 → 27 → 28.** Rings, then aromatic rings, then substituted aromatics.
- **29 → 30.** Constitutional isomerism before stereoisomerism: "different
  connectivity" must be secure before "same connectivity, different arrangement".
- **31 → 33.** CIP priority is taught once, in the E/Z unit, then reused for R/S.

Safe to reorder: units 17 and 18 can swap or move; Stage 10's units 35–37 are
mutually independent.

---

## Where learners actually struggle

Ranked by how much remedial attention they needed:

1. **Parent chain selection** when the longest chain bends or branches on the page.
2. **Numbering direction** — reaching for the tidy substituent locant instead of
   letting the principal group decide.
3. **Amide vs amine**, and the look-alike families generally.
4. **Counting the nitrile carbon.**
5. **Writing locants on locant-free suffixes** (`butan-1-al`).
6. **Ester halves swapped.**
7. **R/S reversal** when the lowest-priority group faces the viewer.

Each of these deserves dedicated error-analysis items — questions that present a
*wrong* name and ask what went wrong. That format proved more diagnostic than
straightforward naming questions.

---

## Suggested build order

If rebuilding content, do **units 9–13 first**, not units 1–3. That block
contains the ladder, the priority concept, and three functional groups — it is
the part that proves whether the pedagogical approach works. Units 1–8 are
comparatively mechanical and can be filled in once the core is validated.
